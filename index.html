01 · The problem

Small pieces, very high speeds

An object in low Earth orbit travels at about 7.5 to 7.6 km/s. At that speed, a fragment the size of a bolt can damage a working satellite. Tracking and predicting these objects is a data problem as much as a physics one.

7.6 km/s

How fast it moves

The dataset's low-orbit rows sit near 7.6 km/s. Orbital speed falls as altitude rises, so higher shells are slower.

100 km

Where objects re-enter

Our cleaning rules treat a perigee at or below 100 km as already re-entered. Drag slows objects with low perigee, so their orbits shrink over time.

Top 10%

What we score

The project labels the highest-decay-risk tenth of the catalog as high risk, then trains models to recognise them from orbit and size features.

02 · Orbit anatomy

Five numbers describe an orbit

The catalog stores these values for every object. Each one changes how long an object stays up and how dangerous it is.

T = 2π √(a³ / μ)    period, with μ = 398 600 km³/s²
v² = μ (2/r − 1/a)    vis-viva speed at distance r

The Phase 3 cleaning step C11 checks that stored periods match Kepler's third law within 1 percent.

Perigee
The lowest point of the orbit, measured as altitude above the surface. Low perigee means more atmospheric drag.
Apogee
The highest point of the orbit. The model does not use it directly, but it sets the shape together with perigee.
Eccentricity
How stretched the orbit is. Zero is a circle. Values near one are very long ellipses.
Inclination
The tilt of the orbit against Earth's equator. Sun-synchronous and many low orbits sit near 53 to 98 degrees.
RCS
Radar cross-section, in m². It describes how strongly an object reflects radar, which roughly tracks its size. The model uses log₁₀ RCS.
Object age
Years since launch, measured to the project's analysis date of 26 September 2026.

    4. Where in orbit the risk sits

    Each cell is an altitude band and an orbit class. The darker the orange, the larger the share of its objects that are high risk. Rows are 250 km altitude steps up to 2,000 km. A dash means no objects fell in that cell.

    What this means in the real world.

    Operators who track satellites screen objects like these to decide whether to move a satellite or plan a re-entry. A missed warning can mean a collision or debris landing somewhere unplanned. A false alarm costs time, fuel and attention. The threshold is where an operator decides how much of each they will accept.

    What this page cannot tell you.

    The "high risk" label is a rule from the data (top ten percent), not a measured collision. The Phase 1 notes describe the Kaggle file as synthetic. These charts show how the method behaves on this data. Scores depend on one training split and one random seed, and they are not a forecast for any real object.

    06 · Learn more

    Questions a reader might ask

    Short answers, with the caveats that matter for this project.

    Why use Spark for about 5,000 rows?

    The dataset is small, and Spark is not faster here. The point was to build a layered pipeline with Delta storage and reproducible tables, the kind of structure that would scale to a larger catalog. The notebooks say this plainly.

    Is the dataset real?

    Phase 1 describes the Kaggle file as synthetic. Its names and values are generated, so very high scores may not carry over to measured catalogs. Treat the results as a demonstration of method.

    What does "high risk" mean here?

    It is a label, not a measured event. An object is high risk when its decay_risk value falls in the top ten percent of the catalog. The models learn to recognise that label from orbit, size and category features.

    Why does the score use log RCS?

    Radar cross-section spans several orders of magnitude, so its distribution is heavily skewed. A log₁₀ transform brings the skew from 4.53 down to −0.27, which suits linear models.
